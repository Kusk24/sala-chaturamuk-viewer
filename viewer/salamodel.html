The point of this capture is the part the real pavilion could never give: rings that look down onto the roof.

The average flatters it; the spread is the honest picture. Across the 38 held-out photographs the score runs from 16.58 dB to 30.58 dB, median 25.93. Those numbers describe the model as trained. The good views are genuinely good — carving, inlaid glass and red steps come back close to the photograph. The worst were veiled by a milky haze in front of the subject: semi-transparent Gaussians floating in the empty space between camera and model. The white cloth behind it carries almost no texture, so nothing in the optimisation pins that space down and a translucent sheet there costs the loss very little. It is the floater failure the outdoor runs met in the sky, with cloth standing in for sky.

What you are looking at has been cleaned, and the cleaning is not free of meaning. The model on this page keeps 77,626 of the 235,390 trained Gaussians. Three cuts were applied to the trained result — no retraining, no second run on the GPU: everything beyond a tight radius of the model's own axis (the haze), everything below the top of the wooden board (the board and the cloth it sat on), and every Gaussian whose largest axis exceeded a size the real surface never needs (the remaining floaters). A gamma lift then raised the midtones, since the object was photographed indoors and reads dark against a black page.

The scores above were not re-measured after that. They belong to the model the optimiser produced, haze included, scored against the held-out photographs the standard way. Cropping changes what is displayed, not what was learned, and re-scoring a cropped model against photographs that still contain a board and a backdrop would be measuring the crop rather than the reconstruction. The honest reading is: these are the numbers for the trained model, and this is the trained model with the empty space taken out of it.

Every photograph registered, and that is the headline. The outdoor captures never managed it: the September pavilion run placed 395 of 446 and filed the interior frames as a separate sub-model that was never trained. Here all 301 frames landed in a single reconstruction. The subject is small, lit evenly and never moved, and the camera walked six complete rings around it — the conditions the capture guide has been asking for since the first attempt folded.

The matching had to change, and the reason was measured rather than assumed. Every earlier run used sequential matching with loop detection off, because the real pavilion is four-faced and vocabulary-tree retrieval cannot tell one face from another. Here the six rings only connect through pairs about sixty frames apart in capture order — frame 30 and frame 91 share 91 verified inliers — which no sequential window reaches, while the ring seams that sequential would have used carry 12 to 25. Sequential would have joined six rings by their weakest joints. Exhaustive matching was safe to use because the four-fold trap does not spring here: a pair ninety degrees apart scores nine inliers, since the wood grain and the folds of the cloth make every direction's background different. On a set this small exhaustive is also simply affordable.

No sky mask. The two previous pavilion runs turn on whether the sky is trained, masked or painted out. Indoors the question does not arise, and the segmentation network that finds sky would have been at real risk of calling the bright white cloth behind the model sky and cutting a hole in the backdrop. The background here is removed geometrically instead, by the same crop and size filter the other runs apply after training.

What it cannot show. The model stands on a board, so its underside was never photographed and nothing in the reconstruction can invent it. The white cloth behind it carries little texture, so what survives the crop there is thin. And this is a model of the pavilion, not the pavilion — it belongs beside the outdoor runs as a controlled comparison, not as a replacement for them.
